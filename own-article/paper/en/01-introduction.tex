\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Greenhouse vegetable production is energy-intensive, and its economic result depends
directly on the quality of climate control. Heating, CO$_2$ enrichment and supplementary
lighting dominate variable cost, and each has to be weighed against the revenue from the
additional yield it buys \cite{katzin2020,katzin2021}. This is the natural setting for
model predictive control (MPC): an economic criterion is optimised over a finite horizon
subject to the temperature, humidity and CO$_2$ bounds that define a productive
microclimate \cite{vanhenten1994,mayne2000,rawlings2012,lin2021}. MPC, however, requires
a process model, and building and calibrating a detailed physical greenhouse model is
laborious, which motivates surrogate models identified from data. Sparse identification
of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) is one such route: the dynamics are recovered explicitly as
a sparse combination of library functions
\cite{brunton2016,brunton2016c,kaiser2018,fasel2022}.
Unlike a neural surrogate, a SINDy model is a white box: its equations can be written out,
checked for physical and dimensional consistency, and embedded analytically in the MPC
solver --- which matters when a controller is to be trusted with a crop.

Whether an interpretable surrogate costs anything in control quality is an empirical
question, and answering it objectively is obstructed by three defects that recur in the
greenhouse-control and data-driven-control literature. First, learned controllers are
often compared against a weak heuristic, whereas well-tuned agronomic rules are a strong
reference \cite{morcego2023,mallick2024,vanlaatum2025}. Second, the reported performance
index is frequently a setpoint-tracking error rather than economic margin
\cite{lin2021,morcego2023}, although the two need not be monotonically related. Third ---
and least obvious --- the hyper-parameters of the surrogate are selected on open-loop
prediction accuracy \cite{kaiser2018,yonezawa2025}, tacitly assuming that a more accurate
model yields better control. Table~\ref{tab:defects} states each defect, the remedy
adopted here, and what we measured the defect to be worth. We emphasise that the first two
defects were present in our \emph{own} earlier analysis of this dataset --- an untuned
reference, and an MPC stage cost whose actuator weights contradicted the prices the
economic criterion itself uses --- and that correcting them removed about two thirds of the
effect we had originally reported.

The third assumption is exactly what the identification-for-control literature warns
against: a model should be judged by the behaviour of the loop it closes, not by the
quality of its open-loop fit \cite{ljung1991,gevers1993,vandenhof1995}. The same
phenomenon reappears for learned models under the name \emph{objective mismatch}: one-step
predictive accuracy is a poor proxy for control performance
\cite{lambert2020,jackson2022,farahmand2017}, and recent surveys confirm that the point
is live for modern data-driven surrogates \cite{wei2023,controlorientedsurvey2025}. That
literature is largely diagnostic, and its prescription --- evaluate candidates in closed
loop --- costs a simulator, a solver and a full season per candidate. The practical
question is therefore narrower and, to our knowledge,
unanswered for economic greenhouse MPC: \emph{if} a surrogate must be selected open-loop,
\emph{which} open-loop criterion tracks closed-loop economics?

A second, related gap concerns the content of the feature library. Physically motivated
basis functions --- saturation vapour pressure, vapour-pressure deficit,
screen-attenuated radiation --- are adopted almost by default in physics-informed
surrogate modelling \cite{raissi2019,karniadakis2021}, on the reasonable presumption that
encoding known physics can only help. They are rarely tested for harm. Yet such features
are nonlinear transforms of the same measured states, hence near-collinear with them, and
collinearity is known to degrade sparse regression \cite{cortiella2021,balanceguided2026}.
Whether that presumption survives a closed-loop economic criterion is the second question
we address.

We address both questions on a validated GreenLight tomato model
\cite{katzin2020,vanlaatum2025}, driven by recorded weather for a single location and
simulated at a 15-min control step. Candidate surrogates were identified on two training
seasons, and thirteen controllers were evaluated --- seven SINDy-MPC variants, a
neural-network MPC, two deep reinforcement-learning agents
\cite{schulman2017,haarnoja2018,raffin2021},
a full-simulator-model MPC and the rule-based heuristic in both
its stock and tuned configurations --- over four held-out test seasons. A \emph{season}
here is the 60-day window from 1 March, and margin is profit per square metre of floor
area. Each learned controller was run at 20 identification replicates per season (10 for
the neural-network MPC); the heuristic is deterministic and was run once per season, and
the full-simulator-model MPC returned only three of the four seasons, its 2022 runs having
exhausted the solver-failure budget. Comparisons use non-parametric signed-rank tests with
Holm correction \cite{wilcoxon1945,holm1979,demsar2006}; against the deterministic
heuristic the test is one-sample against a constant rather than genuinely paired. The
contributions are three.

\textbf{(i) One-step accuracy and multi-step stability rank candidate libraries in
opposite orders, and the closed-loop ordering follows the multi-step one.} The
identification ladder evaluated 72 labelled configurations, covering 54 distinct fits ---
two of the optimizer labels turn out to be bit-identical. Within its degree-1 block ---
the degree-2 candidates are all non-embeddable in the MPC solver --- the library with the
best one-step accuracy is the worst on every multi-step metric. Taking the undenoised
cells under the two sparse estimators ($n = 40$ fits per library), adding physically
motivated features improves
one-step RMSE from $1.86$ to $1.68$~$^\circ$C while degrading the median 24-h rollout RMSE
from $2.67$ to $24.27$~$^\circ$C, raising the regression condition number from $8.2$ to
$53.4$ and the divergence rate from $0.000$ to $0.076$ (Figure~\ref{fig:reversal});
withholding only the bilinear cross terms recovers part of the loss but not the ordering
(rollout $10.58$~$^\circ$C, $\kappa = 24.5$). In closed loop, a raw, physics-free library
--- also the sparsest and the best conditioned of the three --- attains the best
four-season mean margin of the thirteen controllers, $+4.32$~EUR~m$^{-2}$ against $+3.09$
for the best physics-informed variant, ahead in 62 of 80 season~$\times$~replicate pairs
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 7.7 \times 10^{-4}$). The gap is small against the run-to-run
spread --- standard deviations $4.08$ and $2.71$ for a difference of $1.23$ --- and it
reverses in one of the four seasons ($-1.56$ against $+0.27$ in 2021). The library
ordering holds again on four seasons never used at any stage of model or controller
development (four controllers, eight replicates, default objective). Only the two
better-conditioned libraries reached closed loop, so this evidence separates the raw
library from physics-without-cross-terms, not from full physics. Physical motivation of a
basis function is therefore not evidence that it belongs in the library.

\textbf{(ii) A benchmark whose comparison defects have been measured rather than
assumed.} Against the properly tuned heuristic the raw-library controller is better by
$+2.06$~EUR~m$^{-2}$, ahead in 75 of 80 season~$\times$~replicate comparisons
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 6.9 \times 10^{-11}$, Holm correction over a family of 13, which we
declare wherever a corrected level is quoted). Three further SINDy-MPC variants also clear
the tuned heuristic, by smaller margins: the raw library is first, not alone. Against the
untuned reference the same
advantage would read $+5.54$ --- the failure mode this literature is criticised for,
reproduced and quantified on our own data. Likewise, two thirds of what initially
looked like a feature-library effect was in fact a defective MPC stage cost
(Table~\ref{tab:defects}, row 2).

\textbf{(iii) A candidate mechanism put to controlled ablation, and found too small to
carry the effect.} Near-collinear physics features inflate the condition number, and at
the frozen recipe's sparsity threshold the regression deletes the boiler actuator
coefficient in 17 of 20 identification replicates. Knocking that single coefficient back
in is statistically significant but small once the objective is corrected --- median
$+0.21$~EUR~m$^{-2}$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-3}$, 20 replicates in a single
season --- and the complementary knock-out is null. Three further observations rule the
coefficient out as the carrying explanation: the same threshold deletes the term in 9 of
20 replicates of the \emph{raw} library as well; the best physics-informed controller
retains it in all 20 and still trails the raw library by $1.23$~EUR~m$^{-2}$; and
raw-library runs that lost the term still average $+3.48$~EUR~m$^{-2}$, above every
physics-library cell including those that kept it ($+2.36$). We therefore report the
conditioning and multi-step stability of the basis, not any single coefficient, as the
explanation the evidence supports.

We are explicit about what this does not show. The advantage is not Pareto-dominant: the
front holds five points, and the PPO agent attains far fewer constraint violations
($1330$ against $4130$ steps per season) at a lower margin. The surrogate carries no crop
state, so the MPC objective is necessarily a proxy and terminal biomass cannot be priced.
The mechanism experiments are single-season and replication is unequal across controllers.
All results are simulation-only, for one location and one planting date, and closed-loop
margins reproduce within a computing environment but not across environments. The
remainder of
the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the controlled
object, the economic criterion, the data, the controllers and the identification
protocol; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the model-selection ladder, the four-season
economic comparison and the mechanism experiments; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
the implications and the limits; Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.
